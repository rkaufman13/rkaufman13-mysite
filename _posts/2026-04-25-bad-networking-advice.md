*This is the kind of photo that adorns bad advice about networking. I will try to be better.*

Most advice you read about networking is bad and wrong. But networking is if anything more important than ever in a job search. Now that there are websites that will "use AI" to send your resume to thousands of job posts in a day, standing out from the crowd is a minimum requirement.

I'm excited to publish this today after a discussion with some colleagues at work, where we are reading a generally excellent career development book that made some missteps in the chapter about networking. So much advice on networking is so bad, I don't even blame the book author. But it did get me thinking about all the bad networking advice out there and before I knew it I'd written this whole post.

Here are two bad takes on networking and why I think they're wrong. Feel free to yell at me if you disagree.

~~**Make a connection with the person you want something from**~~ Just ask for what you want.
If you want a referral to a job at my company, the worst way you can ask is by pretending to ask for something else. The best way you can ask is to ask.

I get messages all the time from new grads saying "I am about to graduate from Whatever Prestigious Program In Computer Science and I saw you work at My Dream Company. I would love to hear about your journey in engineering." That is at least two lies (sorry, my job is great but nobody dreams about working in martech). Someone told them that they have to make a genuine connection with a networking "target" before asking a favor, but asking me to write you a novel about my life that I know you don't want to read is not how to do it.

If you want a referral, just ask for one. The person might or might not give it to you, but "they buttered me up with a softball question first" is not going to be the thing that tips the scales in your favor. 

If you DO have a genuine connection, fine. One of the best Linkedin messages I got was essentially, "I'm about to graduate from Your Alma Mater with a degree in CS. As an alumni, would you mind referring me to This Specific Job?" With a link to the specific job, and a resume attached.

I've also sent referrals for people who didn't even have that connection. Trust me, it's okay to say, "Hello, I'm interested in This Job at Your Company. I have three years of experience in Important Skill and would be grateful if you'd be open to referring me. Here's my resume."  I'm still going to use my judgment based on the person's resume, but believe me, I'm grateful they asked.

This does not mean you should never ask questions, but that they should be genuine. If I got a genuine question from someone who was clear that they were actually asking, that would be lovely.

I would answer this question in a heartbeat: "Hey, you're a self-taught woman in tech and I'm a self-taught woman in tech, what resources should I use to further my career?" 

Or "You're a woman in tech and I'm a woman in tech, do you recommend I apply for a job at your company or would you say it would be like entering a sexist viper pit?" If the answer was "don't apply" I probably still wouldn't answer a complete stranger; luckily I *can* answer truthfully because the answer would be "Yes, please apply!"

~~**Send news articles to people to stay in touch**~~ If you do this I will block you.

So many articles and books suggest that after you meet someone who could be helpful to your career, you must "stay in touch" by sending them interesting news articles from your industry every quarter or so. I think most people recognize this as cringe but in case anyone thinks this is seriously a good idea, it is not. I'm only half kidding about the blocking thing.

It is good to connect with people, and it is good, if you are job searching or in need of something, to remind them that you exist. I think that is where the seed of this bad advice comes from.

If you are job searching, you are allowed to reach out to your contact and say "Hey, it's been a long time. I'm looking for a job where I can do X Y or Z. Here's a short summary of what I've been up to since we last talked. Please reach out if you can think of anything that fits my skills." If you are in the same geographical area as this person, you can add "I'd love to buy you a coffee and catch up," but again, only if you genuinely want to (and ideally have reason to think they genuinely would want to), otherwise you're just adding a chore to someone's list.

You can (and should) do this whether or not you have "kept in touch" over the intervening time. I recently got a message from someone I last worked with in 2016, and I was happy to hear from her. I didn't need quarterly reminders that she existed over the last decade.

There are two huge exceptions, because I don't want readers to think that they should never send a news article to anyone. First, I love sending unsolicited articles about work stuff to my work friends, emphasis on friends. And they love receiving them (I think). I love it when they send me stuff back. The friendship is what gives us the privilege to send articles; sending articles isn't what grows the friendship.

Second, if you just met someone at a networking event, yes of course it's good to follow up with something like, "It was great to meet you, here's a link to that article we talked about today" or whatever. Solicited messages are great. Performing My Quarterly Relationship Maintenance By Sending You Crap is not.

You can also be visible in ways that don't involve actively putting yourself in someone's inbox. Posting on ~~the torment nexus~~ LinkedIn or your social platform of choice, writing a blog ([oh hey]({{ site_baseurl | relative_url}}{% link _posts/2024-12-21-why-blog.md %})), starting a newsletter, all those boring ways of getting visibility are much more effective and less annoying than individually DMing people articles about Anthropic's latest pricing faux pas to remind them that you exist.
